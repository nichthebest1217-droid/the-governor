by Vincent Liu ‘27

Kind, approachable, and always smiling – that’s the Ms. Anderson students meet in the hallway. The Ms. Anderson on paper, though, tells a different story: she teaches the most brutal course Govs offers: AP Physics C, along with Multivariable Calculus and Advanced Physics. For students who don’t know her, the two sides are hard to reconcile. Behind her engaging and patient teaching hides a content load that has intimidated even the strongest STEM students.

Double-majoring in Math and Physics in college, Ms. Anderson wasn’t sure whether she wanted to be a theoretical physicist or an engineer afterward. After graduation, she initially planned to return to her college as an assistant field hockey coach before heading to graduate school. “I kind of had a feeling I wanted to teach eventually, but I just didn’t quite know how that would work out,” she said. “And it worked out a lot sooner than I thought.” When Govs posted a Physics teacher position opening that May, she applied – and stepped into the classroom at a strikingly young age.

In her Physics C class, Ms. Anderson derives equations step by step from fundamental principles alongside her students. She never took a single education course in college, yet Govs handed her the freedom to build her own teaching style from scratch. “Govs, for me being so new, gave me a lot of freedom, which I was surprised by,” she said. “Govs just kind of threw me into it.” Drawing on her experience as a teaching assistant in college, she now treats each class like an extended tutoring session – turning traditionally lecture-based STEM subjects into discussions.

Ms. Anderson’s suggestion for students approaching her classes is simple: pay attention. She doesn’t think her classes are as hard as their reputation suggests—if students stay consistently engaged during class time. Where students commonly get stuck, she explained, is in applying the cram-the-night-before strategy that works for other subjects. “Physics is all about practice,” she said. “You need to practice and then digest it and then practice.” Her quizzes are designed to test understanding rather than recall, so students used to reviewing notes the night before often find themselves blindsided.

When asked to describe herself in her own words, Ms. Anderson landed on “kind and approachable”—then laughed at the contrast. “It’s mean content,” she said. “It’s a mean class.” Kind teacher, mean class: that, perhaps, is the Ms. Anderson paradox. Behind every equation derived step by step on the whiteboard, behind every answer to a student’s question about a disappearing negative sign, there is a teacher who chose one of the hardest subjects Govs offers—and decided to make it feel like one of the friendliest.
