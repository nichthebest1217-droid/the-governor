by Jason Li ‘26

When I was 10, I got my first crime novel for Christmas: Sherlock Holmes. It was a classic: intriguing, sophisticated, and full of twists that a 10-year-old would never expect. The murderers were more than just “bad people”; they had their own tragedies, and their crimes became acts of desperation. 

This spring, Ms. Davis started a new elective called “True Crime as Novels.” I got to know this class through Cecileah Wang ‘26, who told me the class is different from her past English classes because “This class isn’t really about analyzing the writing style or the historical period, but more like how the killer thinks in that moment.” I interviewed Ms. Davis and asked her some questions about the class. 

JL: What inspired you to teach this class?

Ms. Davis: I loved true crime myself. I’ve watched all the HBO and Netflix true crime shows. I’ve also talked to multiple kids, and they seemed interested in this topic. I thought this class would be a great way to connect true crime with literature. 

JL: What is a book you are teaching?

Ms. Davis: The first book I’m teaching is The Fact of a Body: A Murder and a Memoir, which tells the story of Ricky Langley, who murdered a six-year-old boy in Louisiana in 1992. It follows the author’s perspective as a law student who wants to fight the death penalty. But as she goes deeper into Langley’s case, she realizes she wants him to die, while also exploring her own childhood memory of being abused by her grandfather. 

JL: What is something you find interesting about antagonists in these books?

Ms. Davis: One thing I find interesting about these true crime novels is that the antagonists are more relatable than I expected. These serial killers are not simply black and white, good or evil, guilty or innocent. The reason this book is so uncomfortable is that we get the whole story about the murderer. He has a difficult upbringing and serious mental health issues. There are times when he is humanized, and it feels really uncomfortable. 

JL: How does point of view in these books impact us as readers?

Ms. Davis: Another thing I think is very interesting about good literature is that the author can manipulate you. The book is written from many perspectives. You move from the crime scene, disgusted by the brutality, to the mom who lost her son, feeling angry and sad. You are drawn to wherever the author wants to take you. The POV of each book or documentary depends on the author or director, and how the story is presented really depends on what they want you to know.

JL: Are you teaching this class again in the future?

Ms. Davis: Definitely. The English department requires us to rotate classes every year, but I will definitely come back to this class in the future.
