by Sophie Kelly ‘26 

When people talk about diversity in private boarding schools, race, nationality, and socioeconomic background usually come to mind. One aspect that is often overlooked is age. 

When you step foot into a classroom at Govs, you’ll find 13-year-old “young sheldons,” and reclassed athletes as old as 20, both on non-traditional paths to the same diploma. The timeline to graduation at private schools is drastically different from that at public schools. As a result, classrooms in subjects like languages and math often include students of all grade levels and ages.

To understand how age affects the classroom, I interviewed Jack Couture ‘26 and Tabi Kelly ‘29 on their experiences with age in their Chinese class. For Jack, his time at Govs included upperclassmen who mentored him, “completely changing the course of his high school career.” Now, as an upperclassman, he feels a sense of responsibility for his younger classmates and hopes to have a similar impact on them, as older students once did for him. 

From another lens, age influences classroom participation. Tabi, as a freshman, views age as an advantage for older students in the classroom when it comes to participation. As a younger student, she is more reluctant to participate in classes where upperclassmen are present. She said “older students are more confident and have more experience participating,” allowing them to feel more comfortable answering questions and helping others. Despite recognizing the classroom confidence that comes with age, she says that she does not always feel that pressure in Chinese because everyone is learning and they all make the same mistakes. 

Despite their age differences, both Tabi and Jack respect the fact that seniors and freshmen alike are both trying to navigate a difficult language. Jack said that typically, he sees younger students “struggling to adapt to the new pressures of high school”; however, at Govs, he has been impressed by younger students excelling in these high-pressure environments. For Tabi, sharing a classroom with older students has also changed her perspective: “Being in class with upperclassmen shows that they’re also taking a risk and trying something new.” Seeing both upperclassmen and underclassmen make the same mistakes in the same classes allows for a more integrated community rather than relying on typical high school hierarchy.  

Ultimately, the age gap at Govs adds layers to the private school experience. It encourages mentorship, participation, and exposes students to non-traditional learning experiences. This unusual form of diversity found in private school classrooms allows for a more valuable learning experience. No matter how old a high school student is, we can all learn from each other, both older and younger than ourselves.
