by Adam Krogmeier 27’

Every morning, students slowly get themselves out of bed before stumbling into the first block at 8:15. For many, this is not laziness, it is biology. This biological miscommunication makes it very difficult for adolescents to perform at their highest ability. 

Sleep is one of the most important factors in how well students learn. Teenagers naturally fall asleep later at night and need between eight and ten hours of rest to function properly. This lines up with what science tells us. The CDC states that adolescent brains need 8-10 hours of sleep to undergo a shift in their internal clocks during puberty, pushing their natural sleep and wake cycles later. Asking a teenager to perform complex thinking at 8:15 a.m. is very challenging.  When school starts early in the morning, most students simply are not getting enough time for homework, athletics, dinner, and socializing.

A recent survey conducted among students at Gov’s revealed that a majority feel very tired during first blocks (M/Tu/W/F) and feel much better during their Thursday A block, which starts an hour later. Students feel they will be able to be more alert, motivated, and focused later in the day rather than the early morning hours. Many say they regularly arrive at school having slept fewer than seven hours. 

Mrs. Kingsbury expressed the difficulties with how the schedule is laid out and how it affects Govs students’ academics and afterschool programs. She pointed out that the current schedule does not align with what research shows about teenage sleep needs or the times of day when adolescent brains are most capable of learning. Students are being asked to engage when it neurologically is very difficult to understand and comprehend coursework.

With all the benefits of starting class later, there are factors that halt the deans and others from making this change. Many parents rely on an early start time to fit their own work schedule and a later dismissal could interfere with afterschool activities that require referee availability, maintenance staff, and athletic trainers. With many outside factors affecting this decision, it makes large scale change very hard to implement. 

So what could a solution look like? From the survey, many students believe pushing the start time back even fifteen to forty-five minutes could make a meaningful difference. A later start around 8:45-9:15 would allow students to arrive more rested, more focused, and genuinely ready to learn. For Govs, we could shorten PA to only be an hour with a 25 minute lunch or shorten passing time to 5 minutes. Moving everything up in the day by a couple hours would allow for afternoon programs to finish up on time and the evening routine of dinner and study hall to take place. Academic performance, mental health, and student wellbeing are all directly tied to how much sleep students get. If Govs wants its students performing at their best, it may be time to rethink when the day begins.
