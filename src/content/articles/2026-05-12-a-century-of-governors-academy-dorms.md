Stepping foot on the campus of The Governor’s Academy and taking a walk through all the dorms is a walk through the school’s history. Over a century of campus housing is built into the walls of dorms like Moody, Farmhouse, and Doggett, and according to Ms. Slater, the school’s historian, “these dorms still shape how students live together today.” After sitting down and learning about the dorms’ rich history, it has become clear that the dorms play a huge role in the kind of community they create.

Built in 1914, Moody is one of the oldest dorms on campus. Its small interior gives the dorm a charming and historic feel. Ms. Slater notes that “smaller dorms tend to have tighter communities,” making it easier for students to connect with each other and get a sense of boarding school life. The catch is that it’s “not cost effective to have a bunch of small dorms anymore,” so new facilities tend to mean larger dorms, which makes it hard to recreate that same cozy feel.

The common spaces of these dorms also affect how students interact on a day to day basis. Ms. Slater compared a newer dorm like Doggett with older dorms like Ingham and Eames, suggesting the dorms with better common rooms bring students together by encouraging them to “hang out there, watch movies, whatever.” She went on to say “better common rooms encourage people to actually hang out together, and that builds community.” Each dorm’s common room reflects the priorities of the time when they were built, with Doggett presenting the most current thinking.

With so many older dorms on campus, the facilities staff have a hard time keeping up with what needs fixing. Ms. Slater mentioned that the older dorms’ electrical systems weren’t designed for modern technology, which was the main reason for limiting refrigerators in rooms. Heating and plumbing are also challenges in trying to modernize these dorms. Ms. Slater explained that if you could look into the walls of an older dorm, you would see decades of electrical and plumbing fixes from past facilities crews.

Still, the older dorms come with their own personality that newer dorms can’t replicate. The archives contain a number of artifacts of past dorm traditions: a book of old songs that Moody residents used to write and sing; a cabinet with decades of student signatures from Ambrose House, a former dorm on campus; a paddle from Eames used for activities which would no longer be allowed; and a t-shirt from Ingham, with its notorious “Ingham Country Club” nickname. Many of these traditions have lapsed, but they show that students want to create memories in these dorms, a tradition that lives on today.

Even though campus is constantly changing, the past memories are evident in the buildings that remain. The dorms we are living in at this very moment tell a story of how the school maintains centuries of history in its bricks and mortar.
