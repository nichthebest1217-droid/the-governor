For the past two weeks, students across Govs were asked to wear name tags around campus. However, not all students followed the request. The tags were meant to make it easier for students, faculty, and other members of the Govs community to identify one another, especially as new faculty joined the school. While the request had a clear purpose, students' reactions raised questions about how effective the initiative was.

The Dean’s team introduced the name tags to help members of the community learn each other’s names and encourage more interaction. The decision was partly influenced by the number of new faculty members joining campus. Ms. Gargano explained there was a “good mix of both” goals: helping people learn names and encouraging members of the community to know one another. For a school where students and faculty interact throughout the day, knowing someone’s name can make approaching them easier. Instead of having to ask someone's name, students could simply look at their tag.

However, some students were reluctant to follow the request. Sienna Mercaldi ‘27 said she did not think students took the name tags as seriously as teachers expected.

“I haven’t worn a name tag since elementary school,” Sienna said, explaining that she viewed the requirement as something that did not fit naturally into a high school environment.

Another concern was the physical size of the name tags. Mr. Nelson pointed out that the name tags were very small, making the names difficult to see from far away. If one of the main purposes of the initiative was to make it easier for people to identify one another, the size of the tags could limit how useful they actually were.

Rather than abandoning the goal of creating a welcoming community, Govs could consider other ways to encourage everyone to get to know one another.

Activities that bring together different grades and faculty could create opportunities for people to introduce themselves, such as having more Unity Days or combining advisories with other groups.

Another option could be a peer leadership program, where groups of 10-15 underclassmen are assigned an older student who can help them adjust to campus and answer questions.

Ms. Gargano suggested that the school could try “a different way that is more appealing” in the future. Finding a more natural approach could also still accomplish the school's goal while making students more willing to participate.
