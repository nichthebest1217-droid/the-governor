This past year, all public schools across the state were mandated to prohibit cell phone use during school hours. Despite not having to follow this law, in September, Govs made one thing clear: Cell phone use would no longer dominate life on campus.

When we returned to school, cell phone use was the biggest topic of emphasis. Students were met with technology guidelines, a cell phone policy focus group, and a series of announcements reminding us to be present, put away distractions, and build relationships with people instead of devices.

At first, there was a noticeable shift in cell phone usage in spaces like the dining hall, Wilkie, and the chapel. Students no longer reverted to cell phones when entering these spaces, and the “encourage, not enforce” approach seemed to work.

A few months later, phones have gradually reappeared during lunch and the awkward time before announcements start at morning meeting. Now “no phones in the dining hall” has become more of a joke than a real expectation, leading to the question: if the phone policy isn’t enforced, does it really exist?

From a faculty perspective, the phone policy was initially meant to “inspire, not require” to create healthy habits. The school hoped that this approach would make students voluntarily give up phones rather than being forced to comply with a rule. In an email sent to families, Dr. Quimby noted that the policy was designed to encourage presence and be emotionally engaged with our relationships on campus–“protecting and honoring the things that make Govs special.” Another email sent out by Ms. Davey said that “Faculty and staff are empowered to uphold these expectations,” encouraging faculty to remind students to put their phones away in classes and all school spaces. To further limit phone use, Parents were told to communicate with students only outside of school hours.

As a student, the reality of instituting a phone policy without clear enforcement has no real effect. The lack of reminders has become inconsistent, making the phone policy seem almost optional. At the start of the school year, almost all teachers required phones to be turned in at the start of class. In most of my classes, the number of phones turned in has dwindled to zero. In the end, the phone policy at Govs raises an important question about school rules and responsibility. While the intention was clear, the phone policy has become largely symbolic, reminding us that truly changing how we use phones on campus requires commitment from the entire community. If the school truly wants to see change in how students use their phones, there needs to be either clear and consistent reminders or a group effort from students choosing to be present on their own.
