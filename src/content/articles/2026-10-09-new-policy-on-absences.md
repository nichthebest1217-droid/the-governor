A missed class can happen for many reasons, whether a student oversleeps, has a family emergency, struggles with a mental health issue, or simply forgets an assignment or deadline and does not want to deal with it. With the first month of school already behind them, students are adjusting to the new attendance rules designed to make skipping class harder to get away with. The policy has sparked questions about how the school should balance student accountability with the understanding that some significant reason could be behind a missed class.

Under the new policy created by the dean's team, consequences increase with each unexcused absence. One unexcused absence leads to a Friday night study hall. A second unexcused absence results in a longer Friday night study hall, and three unexcused absences result in "clipboarding.” According to Ms. Gargano, "clipboarding" means checking in on campus on Sunday from 9am to 7pm, signing a sheet every hour to show they are present. Both day students and boarders are expected to follow the same Sunday schedule.

The change in rules is meant to make missed classes and the consequences harder to ignore. Mrs. Kingsbury explained the reasoning behind the change: "Every hour a student misses, they owe us back an hour.” Her comment shows that class time is valuable and that students should take responsibility for what they miss. Teachers are frustrated with the lack of consequences for multiple absences.

Last year, students seemed to take advantage of the fact that three unexcused absences equaled a Friday night study hall. Students would use two of those three unexcused absences to skip a class if they wanted, avoiding accountability. Mrs. Kingsbury said the new rules bring a different kind of responsibility for staff, but not necessarily more work. Instead, she described it as “different levels of commitment to enforce things.”

Some students feel that the policy does not leave enough room before they immediately get in trouble. Ayla Cifric ‘27 and Grace Fyrberg ‘27 said, “We don't particularly think that is fair.” They would prefer a two-strike system. A first absence would serve as their one and only warning, with a Friday night study hall following the second unexcused absence. Their idea would still hold students accountable for missing class while giving them an opportunity to learn from their mistake before facing their punishment.

Although the policy is designed to make skipping harder, its success may depend on whether students feel the consequences are fair. As the year continues, the school will have to see if a stricter punishment will actually change student behavior or simply create a new set of concerns.
