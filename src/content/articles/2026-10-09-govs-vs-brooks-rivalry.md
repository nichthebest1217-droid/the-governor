Almost all Govs students are familiar with our rivalry with Brooks. However, where does that rivalry come from? The Govs-Brooks rivalry is a century old, and the school should make an effort to keep the rivalry vibrant in the years to come.

According to a past *Governor* article from 2010, it states that the first time the two schools played each other was in a hockey game on December 18, 1931. This was only five years after Brooks had been founded in 1926. So how could a school that was founded in 1926 compete so fiercely with our 1763 founding? And who did we compete with before then?

Mrs. Slater, the school historian, shared that originally our rival was Belmont Hill. However, when Govs became co-ed in 1971, it became clear that it would no longer make sense to have our rival be an all-boys school. Peter Bragdon, former head of school and Brooks faculty kid, decided that Brooks would be the new major rival.

The 2010 Governor article also said that Govs and Brooks didn't originally play each other in football as they feared the schools would only play pranks on one another. However, after the ISL formed in 1948, Brooks became a regular on the teams’ schedules.

The relationship between Govs and Brooks involves school spirit, traditions, and a sense of fun that only students can bring. Some of those early traditions were certainly more extreme than what we see today. Govs students used to burn mannequins dressed in Brooks apparel to signify Govs students’ superiority over Brooks students — both academically and athletically. Another tradition involves an athletic competition between the two schools where the losing team would fly the flag of the opposing school that had won. Additionally, students have involved Brooks in many senior pranks, like putting Govs merch all over their school.

The reason the Govs-Brooks rivalry has lasted so long is simple: proximity. Unlike a school like St. Georges, Brooks is close enough to feel like a constant competitor. The two schools are only 11.3 miles apart, and outside of sports, they compete for similar incoming students. They are both boarding schools in northern Massachusetts that prioritize athletics, small class sizes, and a close-knit community.

As of now, the rivalry between Govs and Brooks feels as though it is a low point. We have lost a lot of previous traditions and playing them in sports almost feels like playing any other school.

“Rivalries really encourage school spirit,” said Mrs. Slater. There are bigger crowds at sports games and events. Rivalries are beneficial to sports culture, but they also help to bring a community together. It makes students proud of their school and what they can accomplish.
