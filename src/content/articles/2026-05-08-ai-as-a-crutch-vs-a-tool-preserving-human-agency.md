Academic help was once a dictionary, a friend, or office hours. Now it’s a machine that thinks for you… AI can finish your sentence, rewrite a paragraph, and produce a full argument in seconds. For students, the development of AI has challenged us to consider what role it should play in our education. If it can do the work for us, then what should we be focusing on?

Artificial Intelligence can be effective as a tool after you’ve figured out your direction, but it becomes a crutch when you use it to choose the direction for you. When AI provides the claim, the reasoning, and the voice simultaneously, critical thinking is lost.

The ethical standard for AI use is human authorship. When juniors write their history thesis, they make several intellectual decisions: choosing the question, framing the claim, and selecting the evidence. They take full responsibility for mistakes because the thinking is theirs. AI undermines authorship when people ask for “the answer” and submit the gathered information instantly and directly, which hands over the process and authority for crafting their response.

Education is supposed to build capacity, not just produce output; it’s more than content transfer. Students build judgment through crafting mental models and applying them. They apply their models in school, learn from and refine them based on teacher feedback, and use them to thrive in real-world settings. When one reaches for AI before building that internal model, they skip this developmental work. AI creates a polished page, but you don’t practice the process of producing it.

Schools face a structural problem: students adopt AI quickly, but many classrooms still grade products over process. This mismatch rewards exactly the crutch behavior teachers want to discourage. The better response to this “crutch-boom” is structural change, not surveillance. Teachers could grade outlines, drafts, and revisions rather than just final pages. Classes can add in-class checkpoints or graded discussions with impromptu prompts that reward real thinking. These changes will make authorship easier to practice and harder to fake.

An AI-rich world will not reward whoever writes fastest; instead, it will reward the ability to judge. It will reward those with the ability to say, here is what I think, here is why, and here is what would change my mind. You can be successful with the facilitation tool of AI, but only if you refuse its most tempting offer: the crutch.
