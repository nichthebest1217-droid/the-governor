If you love the popular Netflix show *House of Cards*, you are probably attracted to Congressman Frank Underwood’s cunning and bold manipulation inside Congress. While being amazed by Underwood’s political tactics and ruthlessness, have you ever wondered: how exactly does Congress work?

To know more about Congress, it’s hard to find a more effective way than being a Congressman yourself. Luckily, Harvard Model Congress, also known as HMC, provides an optimal platform. Organized entirely by Harvard students and staff, HMC is held annually for four days in Boston, welcoming high school students domestically and internationally. HMC requires students to assume the role of real-life congressmen, senators, and members of government. Students are required to step into their represented politicians’ shoes even if their personal political views don’t align with those of their politicians.

As a delegate in the Senate Select Committee on Intelligence this year, I deeply felt the emphasis on representing the politician at HMC. For example, my politician, Senator Roger Wicker from Mississippi, leans toward installing more military infrastructure locally to strengthen the US’s control in Africa. Although I personally believed this to be too aggressive, I still spoke in favor of this idea and negotiated with others around it. I also remember arguing against other delegates over whether the government should partner with tech companies to fight foreign cyberattacks. With an equal representation for both parties, the bills that were passed were usually highly bipartisan in my committee.

“People are just nice in person” is what I felt the most in my committee. While some heated debate indeed took place when in session, delegates from my committees were very friendly privately. We talked playfully about the HMCcrushes Instagram account, complained about the disappointing aspects of our school, and cheered each other’s dreams. We also got to ask random questions to our Harvard Undergraduate Committee chairs about the craziest things they had done at Harvard, their SAT/ACT scores, whether they were happier after entering Harvard, and their definition of success. It only took a day or two to get along with most of the people.

Here are some insights from Mr. Satow, who is the new director of HMC, about what he is looking for from applicants:

1. Are you going to be engaged in representing your politician and follow Parliamentary Procedure (don’t worry, no test about this in the application!)
2. Are you going to be comfortable raising your hands in a room full of strangers? Yes, this leans toward extroverted people.

3. If you are more introverted, are you still willing to be active in smaller groups, such as the National Security Council and the President’s Cabinet?

My placard, signed by my committee’s delegates and chairs, stands still on my desk in my room, motivating me constantly. Joining HMC is a decision I’ve never regretted. Apply and put your own placard on the table, future delegates!
