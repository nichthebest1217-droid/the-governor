In the fall of 1971, 26 female students arrived, marking a milestone start to coeducation at Governor Dummer Academy. What motivated GDA to change from 208 years of history? How did the story of girls at Govs begin?

*In Byfield Days* by Webster Bull gives us some answers. According to the book, GDA struggled with a growing financial crisis in 1970. Affected by the Vietnam War and the cultural revolution, the school’s admission applications had continued to go down. As the situation grew increasingly concerning, the school’s board met multiple times to discuss potential solutions, among which included a five-day boarding program, new weekend-off policies, and coeducation.

Initially, coeducation was not a favored proposal. The trustees thought it would lead to extra expenses “the school couldn’t afford.” Some thought Govs should keep being the “only 100% boys’ school.” One trustee even suggested “possibilities of a merger with some school such as Pingree.” Val Wilkie, the headmaster that year, also believed that “there was no pressing need for coeducation.”

Nevertheless, as the financial situation became increasingly strained with admission applications decreasing and income from tuition falling $11,200 short for 1970–1971, meetings were called again. Debates were heated. Finally, the decision to accept girls – but only “girl day students” – was made.

When the girls first arrived, the Boynton House was arranged to be their dressing room, with the second floor’s four dorm rooms transitioning into a smoking lounge, a study hall, and two locker rooms. Anne Mackay-Smith ‘75, looking back at the first few months of school, recalled about the house in 1991: “It was where we went hide. It was hard to be a constant curiosity, and there was a certain amount of pressure. The problem with being a minority – any minority – is that every act becomes emblematic for the entire group.” Aside from the difficulties, however, the girls formed an “unusual fellowship” in those first few months, friends and acquaintances being made even between the most different people.

GDA’s decision of enrolling girls was relatively rushed and last-minute compared to other schools’. As Dick Leavitt, a long-serving math teacher, pointed out, “St. Paul’s used a summer program to experiment with coeducation… We didn’t. We just jumped in.” Mike Moonves, a former admissions director, also recognized that “we didn’t prepare well enough…” Lack of planning contributed to a lot of difficulties. Problems arose for both faculty, some of which had a hard time trying to adapt, and girls who often felt like “they were second-class citizens.” When Peirce first became a girls’ dorm, its first floor bathroom still had urinals in it.

“The girls were very clever,” Leavitt noted, “they put potted plants in the urinals. So, I guess it was trial by fire for all of us – for the girls, for the boys, for the staff.” Indeed, the girls’ positive energy and inspiring courage brought about solutions and changes for the better, making GDA – now Govs – the wonderful community it has become.
