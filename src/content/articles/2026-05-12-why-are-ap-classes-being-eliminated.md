Govs is eliminating Advanced Placement classes in the next two years, a decision that will change how students approach rigorous work. Long seen as the highest academic challenge and a way for seniors to show colleges that they are ready for college-level work, AP courses follow a strict nationwide curriculum created by the College Board to prepare students for standardized exams each May.

Mrs. Gold from the Academic Office said the decision to remove AP classes altogether is intended to give departments and teachers more flexibility in designing challenging courses. Instead of following the strict curriculum set by the College Board, teachers will be able to create their own advanced classes that push students “in ways that are not constrained by the pace and content limitations of the AP curriculum.”

The highest-level classes will now be “Signature Advanced” courses, which will represent the highest level of study in each department and will “require strong preparation, prerequisites, and teacher recommendations.” According to Mrs. Gold, the goal is not to “dilute the rigor but to elevate it.” She explained that while AP courses provide a clear framework, they are also tied to a prescribed curriculum and a high-stakes May exam, which can pressure teachers to move quickly through material and “teach to the test” instead of spending more time on complex ideas and extended projects.

One of the biggest concerns for students about the change is how it will appear on college applications. The academic office says that they will “work closely with the College Counseling Office to communicate the rigor of our Advanced courses in school profiles and counselor letters.” They note that selective colleges evaluate students within the context of their school. When a school does not offer AP courses, students are not penalized; what matters most is that students pursue the most challenging courses available to them and perform well.

Departments have discussed the pros and cons of the change, asking, “If we weren’t constrained by the AP curriculum, what other topics could we explore?” Teachers may keep strong AP content while expanding it, and they will be given professional development time to work on these new classes.

Govs is not alone in changing its AP policy. Several other independent schools — including Milton Academy, Nobles, St. George’s, Middlesex, Phillips Academy, and Phillips Exeter Academy — have also moved away from AP programs to create more flexible advanced classes.

As the transition moves forward, helping families, students, and colleges understand the change will require clear communication and planning. Whether the new approach ultimately benefits students will become clearer in the years to come. For now, the shift represents a significant change in how Govs defines academic rigor, placing more responsibility on departments and teachers to shape what advanced learning will look like for future students.
