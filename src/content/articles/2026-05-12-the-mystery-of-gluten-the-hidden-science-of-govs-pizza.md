What’s your favorite pizza at Govs? Cheese and spinach with tomato is one that has stayed on my list since the first day of my Govs journey. I remember when I took my first bite: the crispy crust cracked softly beneath my teeth and the melted cheese burst across my tongue. Even now, that same taste comes back every time I pass by the pizza station.

But have you ever noticed that sometimes the label says *gluten-free*? That phrase immediately raised a series of questions in my mind. What is gluten? What does it do to our bodies? And most importantly, if something is gluten-free, does that mean the unforgettable

flavor I experienced on my first day of school is somehow changed or even lost?

Here is what I learned about gluten. Gluten is the main storage protein in wheat, mainly consisting of gliadin and glutenin. Although similar proteins–existing in rye, barley, and oats–are also referred to as gluten, the gluten used in or naturally existing in pizza, bread, pasta, and other baked goods—and the health issues related to gluten—are primarily concentrated on wheat.

The crunchy texture of pizza is created by gliadin and glutenin. Gliadins contribute to the viscosity and extensibility of the dough, whereas glutenins give the dough its strength and elasticity. This is why bread rises and forms a bubble structure within it. Therefore, if you enjoy the moisture and crunchy texture of pizza, gluten-free pizza might not be the most optimal choice.

Despite the significant role that gluten plays in improving the texture and structure of baked goods, both researchers and the public have shown growing attention about gluten-led health concerns. Gluten is highly associated with several health issues, including coeliac disease, wheat allergy, and non-coeliac gluten sensitivity. Due to the unique structure of gluten, its peptides are resistant to break down, meaning they are difficult for our gut to digest. As a result, some people may have difficulty processing gluten-containing foods, leading to symptoms such as diarrhea, gas, and bloating. Moreover, about one percent of Western populations have an autoimmune disease—coeliac disease—in which patients’ immune systems target gluten proteins in their small intestine, causing chronic damage to the intestine. People who suffer from wheat allergies should also avoid gluten, and gluten has been linked to non-coeliac gluten sensitivity, although the mechanism is still unclear and debatable.

We should all appreciate the options that the dining hall offers, providing more food choices for people with different needs. Meanwhile, if you do not experience any of the symptoms mentioned above, there is no reason to be afraid of gluten. So the next time you walk past the pizza station, definitely grab a slice or two and enjoy all of the flavor that our dining hall serves.
